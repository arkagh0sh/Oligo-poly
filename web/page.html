Equivariant Gröbner bases

Polynomial rings whose variables are indexed by atoms, computed in your browser.

What the page computes

The variables xa are indexed by the atoms a of a structure: the dense linear order (Q, <), or the dense meet-tree. An automorphism of the structure renames the variables. You give finitely many orbits of polynomials, and the page computes an equivariant Gröbner basis of the ideal that they generate: finitely many orbits B such that a polynomial f is in the ideal if and only if f reduces to zero by elements h of B with supp(h) ⊆ supp(f).

By default, the algorithm is the colouring of Section 5 of A. Ghosh and A. Lopez, Computability of equivariant Gröbner bases (2025). The one-colour variant colours at most one variable of each polynomial; it is often faster.

Writing a generator

Write one generator per line, as description : polynomial. For example,

x < y & y < z : x*y - z

is the orbit of xaxb − xc for all atoms a < b < c. The atoms are the variables of the line. The description must fix the relative position of the atoms completely, so that it defines one orbit. A line without : is a polynomial with the description true, as in x^2 - 1. The sign # starts a comment.

constants and parentheses

For the dense meet-tree, < is the lexicographic order, and there are two more symbols:

le(x, y)
x is below y in the tree, or equal to y
meet(x, y)
the largest point below both x and y

Polynomials

Use + - * / ^ and parentheses. A product can also be written 2x, x y or 2(x + 1). Coefficients are exact rationals, such as 3/2 or 0.25; only constants can divide. A name may have several letters, so xy is one variable.

Reading the result

Each line of the result is one orbit of the basis, written in the same syntax, with atoms x1 < x2 < …. The basis is minimal and its tails are reduced. Monomials are ordered lexicographically, with the largest atom as the most significant variable.

If a description allows no atoms, or several orbits, the page says so; for several orbits, it shows two possible completions. The orbit limit stops computations that grow too large.
